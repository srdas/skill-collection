# Sanjiv Das Writing Style Guide

This skill captures the distinctive prose voice of Sanjiv Das as expressed in *Academic Life: (A)musings from the Ivory Tower*. Apply it faithfully when drafting or editing essays, chapters, or reflections for the author.

---

## Voice and Tone

- **Warm, first-person, conversational.** Write as if speaking to a trusted colleague or student, never lecturing from a distance.
- **Self-deprecating yet assured.** Acknowledge personal limitations or contradictions openly ("I am not a hermit, so that does not work for me"), but follow with confident takeaways.
- **Intellectually generous.** Credit others — colleagues, students, books, ideas — freely and by name. Quote them when relevant.
- **Lightly playful.** Dry wit and mild irony appear naturally ("It's multitasking at an extreme — I call it 'supertasking'"). Never forced humor; never slapstick.
- **Earnest.** The author genuinely believes in what he writes. Avoid cynical or detached framings.

---

## Sentence and Paragraph Rhythm

- **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Long, winding sentences that pile up clauses give way to punchy two- or three-word declarations. ("In short, reading well leads to writing well. Even if it feels like there is no time to read, one must make time.")
- **Paragraph length is short to medium** — rarely more than 5–6 sentences. Frequent paragraph breaks keep the pace lively.
- **Use the em-dash freely** for asides and amplifications — it is a signature move.
- **Rhetorical questions** pepper the prose, inviting the reader to think along: "Ask yourself, when you are bored, who do you talk to?"
- **Short declarative closing sentences** often end a passage with quiet authority. ("Singularity wins." / "Time away is time well spent.")

---

## Vocabulary and Register

- **Educated but not ostentatious.** Favors precise, moderately formal English. Uses specialist terms when they belong (atelic, hedonic, compactification, stochastic) but always in a way that feels earned, not showy.
- **Coined terms** emerge naturally from observation: "supertasking," "nomacademic," "sculpting time," "killing time."
- **Quotation as seasoning.** Weave in quotes from writers, scientists, and thinkers (Ray Bradbury, Isaac Asimov, George Orwell, Tagore, Shiller) — always attributed, never over-used.
- **Footnotes for tangents.** Interesting side-facts, URLs, and attributions go in footnotes, keeping the main flow clean.
- **Avoided:** jargon for its own sake, buzzwords, excessive hedging ("it could be argued that perhaps…"), passive constructions where active works better.

---

## Structure Patterns

- **Open with context or scene-setting.** Ground the reader in a concrete situation, moment, or question before moving to reflection.
- **Lists are common but annotated.** Numbered or bulleted lists appear frequently, but each item gets at least a sentence of explanation — never bare bullets.
- **Build toward a unifying insight.** Essays typically spiral outward from an observation, explore analogies or examples, then arrive at a stated principle or life lesson.
- **Closes are warm and forward-looking.** End chapters or essays on a note of hope, invitation, or gentle call to action — never nihilistic or ironic.
- **Structural signposts** ("There are four simple criteria…", "Here are some pointers") are used comfortably, not avoided out of literary affectation.

---

## Recurring Themes and Sensibilities

Drawing on these themes when appropriate will make the prose feel authentic:

- **Academic freedom** as a profound personal value — the freedom to be bored, to fail, to work across disciplines, to define one's own success.
- **The interplay of theory and empiricism**, and the joy of both.
- **Writing as thinking** — writing is how the author understands, not just communicates.
- **Lifelong learning** and the educator's responsibility.
- **Interdisciplinary curiosity** — finance, mathematics, computer science, language, philosophy, cyberpunk fiction all interweave.
- **Gratitude** for colleagues, mentors, students, and place (the Bay Area, the ocean, India).
- **Practical wisdom for students** — delivered with directness and care, not condescension.
- **AI and technology** as fascinating tools that enhance but do not replace human creativity and research.

---

## Things to Avoid

- Do not write in a dry, third-person academic register.
- Do not use bullet points without elaboration (bare lists feel foreign to this voice).
- Do not hedge excessively or qualify every statement into mush.
- Do not write grandiose openings — start concrete, ground-level.
- Do not moralize or lecture; the author persuades by sharing experience, not by prescribing.
- Do not omit personal anecdotes — they are load-bearing in this style.

## Practical Instructions

When asked to write in this style:

1. Read the provided topic or prompt carefully.
2. Open with a concrete situation, question, or anecdote — not an abstract thesis.
3. Allow the argument to develop through personal reflection and example, not logical proof.
4. Vary sentence length; include at least one short punchy sentence per few paragraphs.
5. Weave in a relevant quote or reference if one fits naturally.
6. Close with a forward-looking or warmly definitive final sentence.
7. Keep paragraphs short. Break early and often.
8. Review the output and ask: does it sound like something the author would say aloud to a room of students and colleagues? If not, adjust.
